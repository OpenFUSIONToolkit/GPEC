GPEC computes the neoclassical toroidal viscosity (NTV) torque, the torque a non-axisymmetric field perturbation δB exerts on the plasma through kinetic resonances. Today it averages δB over each particle's bounce motion while holding the particle on one flux surface. This milestone (M2) adds a second method that takes the same average along the particle's real guiding-center orbit, which drifts off the surface. In GPEC's KineticForces module the existing surface method is called fgar and the new orbit method forb; this report uses those names. forb must reduce to fgar as the orbit width goes to zero, the zero-orbit-width (ZOW) limit. At finite orbit width (FOW) it is the new physics this project is for.

In brief. The ZOW limit works: the difference between the two methods falls as a power law in orbit width with no floor. It falls more slowly than the plan's target assumed. At the physical orbit width the torque is not determined, because a numerical patch at the trapped–passing boundary, the "layer fill" described in Section 1, sets it rather than the orbits. I propose a fix. Attempt 2 is the last M2 attempt under the stop rules, so I stop here for your answers.

Where the decisions are. Section 1 explains what the code does; read it first. Each later section ends in its decision card.

1. What M2 computes

The question. When the bounce average is taken along the real orbit instead of on a flux surface, and fed through GPEC's existing resonance operator and energy integral, does the torque return to the surface torque as the orbit width goes to zero, and how fast? Is the finite-width answer converged?

The torque in one paragraph. A particle resonates with the perturbation when ℓωb − n(ωD + ωE) ≈ 0. Here ωb is its bounce frequency (the transit frequency if it circulates), ℓ an integer bounce harmonic, ωD its toroidal precession from magnetic drifts and ωE the E×B rotation frequency. The strength of each resonance is |δJℓ|², the ℓ-th bounce harmonic of the perturbation averaged along the particle's path. δJℓ is built from δB/B and ∇·ξ⊥, where ξ is the plasma displacement. Collisions enter as a Krook frequency ν. GPEC returns a complex T whose real part is the torque (N·m) and whose imaginary part is 2n δWK, with δWK the kinetic energy of the perturbation. Particles are labelled by x = E/T, the kinetic energy over the temperature (u = √x is used as the table coordinate), and by the pitch λ = μB₀/E, with μ the magnetic moment and B₀ a reference field. λ stays fixed along an orbit, and v∥² ∝ 1 − λB/B₀, with v∥ the velocity along the field.

What changed in the code. Figure 2 shows where forb sits. It is a new file, OrbitTorque.jl, switched on by the deck key forb_flag (default off); orbit_nlambda (128) and orbit_nenergy (12) set its table size. The orbits come from the integrator built in M1, the previous milestone. Each key has a case in the test that runs the same problem from an input deck and from the scripting API and checks they agree. Two things stay at their ZOW form until M3, the next milestone, as planned: the drive ∂f/∂ψ (f the equilibrium distribution) is read at the start surface ψ₀, and the phase-space volume element uses ψ₀ in place of the canonical toroidal momentum Pφ.

How the orbit table is built. On each flux surface ψ₀, forb launches one orbit for every pitch node, every energy node and both signs σ, and records ωb, ⟨φ̇⟩ and |δJℓ|² for ℓ = −6…6. Figure 3 gives the rules. An orbit is trapped or passing; lost if it leaves the plasma or reaches the magnetic axis grid; or unclosed if it has not returned by the integrator's time limit τmax. The last step, the layer fill, overwrites every entry with |λ − λtp| < 10·|λc − λtp| with the surface value. Here λc is the orbit's own trapped–passing boundary on that energy row, found by bisection. It differs from λtp because the orbit drifts off ψ₀. I added the layer to avoid the poorly resolved strip between λc and λtp. The factor 10 has no physical meaning.

2. The zero-orbit-width limit (check 1)

How the width is scanned. The scan changes Z, the species charge number (GPEC's zi; the particle carries charge Z·e). Everything else stays: the particle is still a deuteron, and temperature, density, ν and ωE come from the same profiles at every Z. Only Z = 1 is a real plasma. Higher Z makes the orbit narrower without touching the equilibrium, the perturbation or the ψ grid, so the ZOW limit can be approached under control.

The physical width at ψN = 0.5 is Δb/a = 0.047. Each factor of 4 in Z divides it by 4, so Z = 4096 reaches 1.1×10⁻⁵. Seven points over 3.5 decades are what it takes to tell a power law from a floor. A wrong phase, Jacobian or sign would leave a floor.

What it shows. The difference falls from 4.5% at Δb/a = 3×10⁻³ to 6×10⁻⁵ at 1.1×10⁻⁵, with no plateau. The fitted slope is 1.18 for complex T and 1.37 for the torque alone. The same orbit table with the surface values put in reproduces fgar to roundoff (Section 4), and M1's toroidal sign map and phase convention agree with fgar for every ℓ. All of this is for one sign s of the poloidal field component Bθ, s = −1, which both test equilibria have; s = +1 is first exercised in M4, on the ARC V3A reactor-design equilibrium. Because the reference changes with Z, the slope assumes the error's prefactor changes little along the scan. The slopes between neighbouring points show no drift:

The absolute values carry ψ-quadrature uncertainty: on 36 nodes instead of 72 the Z = 16 difference is 5.7%, not 4.5%, because the difference lives in narrow edge features. A cleaner scan would also divide ωE and ν by Z, so every low frequency keeps its ratio to ωD and the reference stays nearly fixed. That is my inference, untested, and a script-only change.

Why first order, and why the 10⁻⁴ target fails. The PLAN.md target assumed the difference would be second order in width. M1 showed the orbit-frequency errors are odd in σ, so they cancel to second order when both signs are summed. A first-order residual needs a first-order source, and M2 has four by design: (i) the drive read at ψ₀ instead of the orbit's mean ψ, written ⟨ψ⟩; (ii) the ZOW phase-space volume; (iii) lost orbits counted as zero, whose share falls with width (24% of entries at Z = 1, 5.5% at Z = 16, 0.2% at Z = 1024, mostly on the edge surfaces where the torque is); (iv) the layer fill. A run at Z = 16 with the layer factor varied rules out (iv). Which of (i)–(iii) dominates is untested; I infer (i) and (ii), which M3 makes exact. A slope near 2 after M3 would mean the orbit average itself is second order, as Liu 2014 finds for the precession resonance. A slope near 1 would mean a real first-order effect, such as the passing-particle label shift M1 found. Either is a result for the paper.

The result. At Z = 1, doubling the pitch grid (128 → 256) changes the forb torque by 2.5% and doubling the energy grid (12 → 24) by 0.7%, so the grids look converged. But the layer factor, which should not matter, moves it a lot:

At Z = 16 the same change moves the difference from fgar only between 5.5% and 5.9%. Figure 7 shows where the two methods differ at physical width. It is not a result; it only locates the problem at the edge.

Why the layer decides it. Figure 8 maps the orbit class on one edge surface, ψN = 0.96, with the layer band drawn on top. At Z = 1 the band is wide, and it also covers lost orbits, so it turns zeros into surface values. Of the 3072 table entries on this surface, 1040 (34%) are lost.

Even at the lowest energy the band covers 14% of the nodes, because the grid packs them at λtp (Figure 4a). The same packing is why doubling the pitch grid could not detect the problem: it refines λtp, not λc or the lost edges.

Reading. Evidence: on this surface, outside the band, the orbit's own Σ|δJ|² is 0.27–0.38 of the surface value for passing orbits and 0.13–0.18 for trapped ones, so the layer pastes in values 3–8 times larger, and pastes them over the lost zeros too. That matches the direction of the table above: the Z = 1 torque moves toward fgar as the band widens. Inference, from one surface and two energy rows: the true Z = 1 forb torque at M2's level of physics is below the layer-10 value of 0.36 N·m; the data do not say by how much. The integrand near λc is smooth enough that nodes packed there resolve it, so the layer is not needed.

The proposed fix

  1. Pitch grid from the orbit's class edges (Figures 10b, 11b), replacing the layer. Lost orbits stay zero until M3 brings the loss boundary. The first version of this card said "pack at λc"; Figure 8a shows that is not enough, because for σ = +1 the edges that matter are passing–lost and trapped–lost.
  2. Energy integral done once per pitch node. The integrals rebuild the energy spline and re-scan 401 points for poles at every pitch they evaluate. On the small TJ test equilibrium (GPEC's TJ_kinetic_NTV_example), the deck-and-API test with forb on takes 22 min on 2 threads, against 45 s without; a profile of one surface puts the orbits at 1.2 s of 5.7 s. A test that slow cannot stay in the suite. The orbit tables themselves cost 130 core-seconds per surface (180 at Z = 1), about 40–60 ms per orbit, mostly in sampling δJ.
  3. Rerun check 4 at Z = 1, and the scan, with more ψ nodes at the edge.

Open design question. Per-row pitch grids do not line up across energy rows, but the energy integral needs values at a fixed pitch coordinate. The plan is a coordinate t ∈ (0, 1) inside each class segment, with nodes at fixed t. That works while each class is one interval whose ends move smoothly with u. Figure 8a breaks this: for σ = +1 the passing class ends between u = 1.4 and 1.9, so at fixed t the energy integrand has a step there. The energy integral then also needs the u at which each class ends, found by bisection like λc. The first step of attempt 2 is to plot the integrand against u at fixed t on this surface, before writing code.

4. The kinetic energy on the orbit path (checks 2 and 3)

On the surface path, δWK on each surface is an m×m′ matrix over poloidal harmonics m, m′, built from ξ on that surface. The kinetic version of GPEC's ideal-stability solver, ForceFreeStates, integrates an ordinary differential equation (ODE) surface by surface and needs that local matrix. An orbit started at ψ₀ reads ξ across its whole width, so on the orbit path δWK couples ψ₀ to every ψ′ the orbit visits (Figure 12): it is a kernel, not a matrix on each surface. Check 2 (the n → −n matrices are conjugate) therefore has nothing to act on.

What the path does give is the total δWK = Im T/2n and the torque for a given perturbation. Check 3 (torque = 2n Im δWK) holds by construction for these scalars, since both come from the same complex T. What can be tested is that the operator is unchanged: feeding the surface values through forb's energy and pitch integrals reproduces fgar to {{VAR_REPLAY}} on every ψ node at physical width. Two code paths (the ψ-quadrature call and a direct table build) give bit-identical tables and torques, serial and threaded.

Q10 - δWK as a scalar on the orbit path (not gating)

Found. On the orbit path δWK is a ψ–ψ′ kernel with no per-surface matrix (Figure 12), so it cannot feed the ForceFreeStates kinetic ODE. PLAN's methods figure ("the dominant δWK matrix element") and checks 2–3 assume it can.

Why it is yours. It changes the y axis of a figure in PLAN, and it keeps the orbit path to torque and energy on a given perturbation, with no self-consistent kinetic response.
