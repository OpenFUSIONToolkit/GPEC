1. What attempt 2 computes

The question. GPEC's KineticForces module computes the neoclassical toroidal viscosity (NTV) torque, the torque that a non-axisymmetric field perturbation exerts on the plasma through kinetic resonances. Its surface method fgar averages the perturbation over each particle's bounce motion while holding the particle on its start flux surface. The orbit method forb, added in M2, takes the same average along the particle's real guiding-center orbit, which drifts off that surface by the orbit width Δb. Attempt 1 left the torque at physical width set by a numerical patch (the "layer fill") instead of the orbits. Attempt 2 removes the patch, as you chose in Q9b (true finite orbit width, FOW, for every class and case), and asks two things again: does forb return to fgar as Δb → 0, the zero-orbit-width (ZOW) limit, and is the torque at physical width converged?

The torque in one paragraph. A particle with kinetic energy E, magnetic moment μ and sign σ of its parallel velocity v∥ is labelled by x = E/T (T the temperature), by u = √x, and by the pitch λ = μB₀/E (B₀ a reference field). It resonates with the n-th toroidal harmonic when Ωℓ = ℓωb − n⟨φ̇⟩ ≈ 0. Here ωb is the bounce frequency (the poloidal transit frequency for a passing particle), ℓ an integer bounce harmonic and ⟨φ̇⟩ the orbit's mean toroidal rate. The surface method splits ⟨φ̇⟩ into the E×B rotation ωE, the magnetic precession ωDx and, for passing particles, the transit term qωb (q the safety factor). The resonance has strength |δJℓ|², the ℓ-th bounce harmonic of the perturbed action, and is broadened by a Krook collision frequency ν. GPEC returns a complex T per unit normalized flux ψ: the real part is the torque density (N·m), the imaginary part 2n times the kinetic energy density δWK, so every comparison below on the complex T covers both. The pitch and energy integrals run in forb exactly as in fgar: adaptive Gauss–Kronrod in λ, and in u an integral whose resonance poles are located exactly on each spline cell (attempt-2 step 1). Only the inputs to those integrals, ωb, ⟨φ̇⟩ and |δJℓ|², come from orbits.

What changed in the code. Attempt 1 tabulated those inputs on a fixed (λ, u) grid and overwrote a band around the trapped–passing boundary with surface values. Attempt 2 builds the table one pitch column at a time (Figures 1 and 3). For each pitch node λi and each σ, 24 orbits along u find the orbit class at each energy: trapped (v∥ changes sign), passing, lost (reaches the edge or the magnetic-axis grid, carried as zero, as you chose in Q9b) or unclosed (has not returned by the time limit, retried once at ten times it). Each class change is bisected to Δu < 6.5×10⁻³. Each trapped or passing interval [ua, ub], a segment, is then sampled at Chebyshev nodes, and nodes are added until a cubic spline through every other node reproduces the rest to orbit_energy_tol (5×10⁻³ in the runs here) of each quantity's largest value. In λ, adjacent columns with the same ordered list of segment classes form a group; within a group the segment ends and the resampled values are splined in λ, and the λ quadrature breaks at the group boundaries. Two new deck keys, orbit_energy_tol and orbit_umax (the largest u, 6.5), join orbit_nlambda and orbit_nenergy; each has a case in the deck-and-API parity test. Nothing outside KineticForces changed (gpec commits b0fb40d14 and 84b09315b).

Your questions on Q9b. You asked whether tables this coarse in u can capture sharp resonances, how sharp class boundaries are handled, whether the stepped boundary of attempt 1's Figure 11 matters, and whether a resonance could hide at a point such as (λ, u) = (0.19, 5.2).

The resonance itself is never on the table. The table holds ωb/u, the precession and |δJℓ|², which vary smoothly inside a segment, and the energy integral finds the zeros of Ωℓ(u) on every spline cell at every quadrature pitch, as fgar does. A resonance at (0.19, 5.2) is therefore found by root-finding, not sampled. What the table must resolve is the inputs, and attempt 1 did not: Figure 2(d) shows its 12 fixed nodes missing a peak in |δJℓ|² next to a class edge. The refined nodes follow it.

Class boundaries are found per column by bisection and integrated as separate segments, so no spline crosses one. In λ the boundary is interpolated within a group; between groups the quadrature breaks halfway between nodes, which is where a stepped error survives. Its size is part of check 4 (Section 3).

2. The zero-orbit-width limit (check 1)

How it is tested. The orbit width shrinks as 1/Z when the charge number Z of the thermal deuterium is raised at fixed mass, temperature and field, while ωb and the perturbation stay the same. The scan runs Z = 1, 4, 16, …, 65536 on the DIII-D-like n = 1 case, on 54 ψ nodes (3-point Gauss–Legendre on the panels of the ψ quadrature, with extra panels above ψ = 0.9), with 64 pitch nodes, orbit_energy_tol = 5×10⁻³ and ℓ = −6…6. At each node forb is compared with the replay of Section 4, so the common pitch error cancels, and the relative difference of the ψ-integrated complex torque is reported. In variant A only Z changes, so the precession ωD ∝ 1/Z fades against the fixed ωE and ν. In variant B, which you asked for in Q9a, ωE and ν are also divided by Z, so every frequency except ωb scales together and the precession resonance keeps its place as the width shrinks.

What it shows. Variant A falls as a power law of slope 0.94, from 3.2×10⁻² at Δb/a = 2.9×10⁻³ (Z = 16) to 6.3×10⁻⁴ at 4.5×10⁻⁵ (Z = 1024), and then stops at 1.8×10⁻⁴ for Z = 4096, 16384 and 65536. Variant B follows A to Z = 16 and then sits at 2.9–4.7×10⁻² at every narrower width. At Z = 4 the orbits are still wide (Δb/a = 1.2×10⁻²) and both variants differ by 30–95%, as they should. Neither variant meets "no floor over 3 decades", and the attempt-1 curve, which seemed to keep falling, was measured against fgar with the pitch error and the layer fill both in it. Figure {{N_FLOOR}} shows where the floors sit in ψ.

Where each floor comes from. I rebuilt the forb table at the floor nodes and swapped one orbit quantity at a time for its surface value, keeping the orbit's segments, nodes and everything else. Table 1 gives the result. Both floors are trapped-particle effects; the passing share at these nodes is 10⁻⁵ of the trapped one in variant B and follows 1/Z in variant A.

The orbit evaluates δJℓ as a 256-point sum over one orbit period; the surface evaluates the same integral on its own θ grid. Both are discretizations of one ZOW quantity, and their difference does not depend on Δb.

What I infer, labelled as such. The limit itself works: below Δb/a ≈ 3×10⁻³ the orbit correction falls in proportion to the width, as a first-order finite-width correction should. Both floors are disagreements between two numerical evaluations of the same ZOW quantities, the orbit's and the surface's, not orbit physics, since neither moves with Z. I have not yet shown which evaluation is the more accurate. The test is a convergence pair at the floor nodes: the orbit with 1024 samples and rtol 10⁻¹⁰ against the surface with four times its θ points, and the two field representations' ∂B/∂ψ compared at ψ = 0.92, since a 1.5% offset at one surface and none at another points at the equilibrium derivatives rather than the orbit (an inference). That would decide whether the fix belongs in forb or is a limit of fgar. The variant-B floor also matters physically: alphas in M3 precess far faster than thermal ions, so a 1–10% precession difference next to the trapped–passing boundary is the kind of error KR1 would carry.

3. Physical width: convergence and cost (check 4)

How it is tested. Check 4, as Q9b set it, asks for no ZOW fallback anywhere, a Z = 1 torque that changes by less than 5% when the pitch and energy resolution are doubled, and a deck-and-API parity test that runs in under 2 minutes. There is no fallback left: every trapped and passing orbit carries its own values, lost orbits carry zero, and the layer fill is gone. Doubling here means orbit_nlambda 32 → 64 → 128 with orbit_energy_tol halved each time, 1×10⁻² → 5×10⁻³ → 2.5×10⁻³, at ψ = 0.5, 0.7 and 0.96 (ℓ = −2…2).

What it shows. The torque converges. From 32 to 64 nodes it moves 7.0%, 2.3% and 3.3%; from 64 to 128, 2.7%, 1.1% and 1.3%. The check passes from 64 nodes and 5×10⁻³, the setting of every scan in this report; the 32-node row fails at ψ = 0.5. The change falls by 2–2.6× per doubling, so extrapolating, the 64-node torque is within about 4.5% of its converged value at the worst of the three surfaces (ψ = 0.5) and the 128-node torque within about 2%. The cost is the table: 2–3 minutes per surface on one thread at 64 nodes (5–6 at 128), against 13–22 s for the integrals. The Z = 1 scan of Section 5 took 5.1 h of table time on one CPU per chunk (four chunks in parallel), 57% of it on the five surfaces inside ψ = 0.13, where the orbits are wide against their distance to the axis; the slowest, ψ = 0.045, took 70 min.

The parity test fails the time limit. The forb case of the parity test runs the TJ example on ψ = 0.49–0.51 with 4 pitch nodes, 15 surfaces in the ψ quadrature. It spent 845 s in KineticForces and 966 s in all, once from the deck and once from the API (job 139226; the test file took 35 min). With the potential off, one of its surfaces costs 5 s of table and under 1 s of integrals (job 139280), about 90 s for the case. But the deck leaves orbit_potential at its default, on, as you chose in Q8, and with Φ in the orbit Hamiltonian a surface costs 150–170 s (job 139284). The cost has a cause that matters more than the time: 65 of the 192 class probes per surface are unclosed after the retry and none is trapped, so each runs twice to the time limit and the table has no trapped segment at all.

4. The replay identity and fgar's own pitch resolution

What the replay is. Every comparison in this report is between forb and a replay: the surface method's own ωb, ωD and |δJℓ|², copied into an orbit table with one full-range segment per column and integrated by forb's integrals on the same pitch nodes. The replay therefore has exactly forb's pitch and energy discretization and none of its orbits, so forb − replay isolates what the orbits change. Q10 retired checks 2 and 3 and kept the replay identity, replay = fgar "to roundoff", as a unit test.

What changed. With attempt 2's groups the identity no longer holds to roundoff, and should not: fgar splines its inputs across the trapped–passing boundary as one curve, while the replay puts trapped and passing nodes in separate groups and breaks the quadrature at λtp. They agree to the pitch discretization error, and Figure {{N_PITCH}} measures it. Against a 512-node fgar reference, both fgar and the replay on the default 128 nodes are off by 8×10⁻³, 2.5×10⁻² and 5×10⁻⁴ at ψ = 0.5, 0.7 and 0.96; on 512 nodes the replay is within 1×10⁻⁵, 2×10⁻⁶ and 3×10⁻⁷. The two schemes converge to the same answer and differ, at equal nodes, by much less than either's error. The unit test is now "replay = fgar to 10⁻⁴ on 512 nodes" (test/runtests_orbits.jl).

A finding for develop, not this project. fgar's default 128 pitch nodes leave a 2.5% error in the torque density at ψ = 0.7 in this case. It does not affect any result here, because the replay shares it. I have parked it in the journal for a separate issue on develop.

Panel (a) also corrects the journal. Splining the energy integral in λ (dashed) and splining the inputs (solid) give nearly the same error at equal nodes. The failures that prompted the redesign came from a test that compared 32 nodes against 128, not from the scheme. The redesign stands on principle: an energy integral splined in λ tabulates the resonance, which sharpens as ν and ωE shrink (variant B in Section 2).

5. The physical-width torque, and how to proceed

What it is. At Z = 1, the physical charge, the same scan gives the first converged finite-orbit-width torque profile for thermal deuterium in the DIII-D-like case (Figure {{N_PROFILE}}). This is not yet a KR2 result: the drive ∂f/∂ψ is still read at the start surface (Pφ enters in M3), lost orbits carry zero by design (Q9b), and the potential is off.

What it shows. Integrated over ψ, fgar gives 0.536 + 0.169i N·m (M0's run of the same case gave 0.554 N·m for the real part) and forb 0.204 − 0.037i: the orbits reduce the torque by a factor of 2.6 and flip the sign of δWK. Inside ψ = 0.9 the change is 15% (0.070 → 0.059 N·m); above it, where 87% of the surface torque sits, it is a factor 3.2 (0.466 → 0.145). The edge peaks at ψ = 0.96 and 0.99 are the ones that shrink most. Part of that is the lost-orbit rule, not orbit averaging: the lost share of the class probes rises from 0 inside ψ = 0.2 to 20% at 0.78, 37% at 0.93 and 48% at 0.99, and those orbits carry zero by design (Q9b). Separating the two effects needs M3's loss-boundary treatment, so I draw no KR2 conclusion here beyond this: the premise, an order-unity orbit effect at the edge, holds in the first converged calculation.
